Where the word "cloud" comes from

Long before anyone sold "cloud computing", engineers drew network diagrams. Machine A talks to machine B, and somewhere between them sits a mess — cables, telephone exchanges, other companies' networks — that matters enormously but is not what the diagram is about. So they drew a fluffy cloud shape around it.

The precise engineering meaning of that squiggle: "a bunch of infrastructure that matters, but which I am deliberately choosing not to explain right now." This course has exactly one job: to erase that squiggle.

So what is actually inside it?

"Someone else's computer" — true, and useless. Whose computer? Where? How many? The real answer is warehouses: buildings full of ordinary machines, most of them individually less impressive than your laptop.

  • Example from class: Google's data centre in Council Bluffs, Iowa — row upon row of servers that exist only to talk to one another.
  • Why Iowa? Not because the users are there. Because cheap electricity, cool air and existing fibre routes are there.
  • Rule to carry forward: data centres get built where the power and the cables already run — not where the people are. This explains a great deal of network behaviour later in the course.

The pivot — the actual point of Lecture 1

We asked where the cloud is, answered it, and the answer turned out to be boring: warehouses in Iowa. That was deliberate. The interesting question was never where the computers are. It is how they talk to each other.

AWS, Azure and GCP are not selling you computers — anyone can buy a computer. They are selling the network that makes a hundred thousand ordinary machines behave like a single one. Take the network away and a data centre is not a cloud; it is an expensive room that happens to be warm.
The one-line mental model for your notebook 
Cloud = Data centre (many ordinary computers)  +  a very fast, very reliable NETWORK
         ^ commodity, anyone can buy it          ^ THIS is the actual product

Networks are not new — the first message was a typo

In 1969, a team at UCLA led by Leonard Kleinrock tried to send the word "LOGIN" to a machine at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) in Menlo Park — the second node of what was then ARPANET.

The cable era

Early machine rooms were held together by hand-labelled cable runs and "DO NOT TOUCH" sticky notes. This is not merely a historical curiosity — a modern and considerably more expensive version of the same problem reappears in the GPU-cluster tab.

The internet is a "network of networks"

There is no single company that owns "the internet". It is thousands of independently owned networks — ISPs, universities, companies, content providers — that agree to interconnect. This is why the syllabus phrases Lecture 1 as "Internet as a network of networks".

Layer of the map

Network edge

The end systems (hosts) that actually run applications — your phone, laptop, smart TV, and the servers at the far end. The edge is where data is created and consumed.

Layer of the map

Access network

The first hop that connects an end system to the rest of the internet: home WiFi + router, fibre/DSL/cable to your ISP, or a 4G/5G cellular link. This is what your "300 Mbps plan" actually buys.

Layer of the map

Network core

The mesh of interconnected routers between the edges. It forwards packets hop by hop and makes no promises. Lecture 2 is entirely about what happens here.

Why this three-way split matters for the exam: when a scenario question asks "where is the problem?", the answer is almost always which of these three — a slow end system, a weak access link (home WiFi), or congestion in the core / at a peering point. The Netflix-buffering scenario is exactly this question.

Latency intuition — build it now, calculate it in Lecture 2

  • Distance costs time, and no money fixes it. Light in fibre travels ~2 × 10⁸ m/s. India → UK is ~45 ms one way before a single router does anything.
  • Bandwidth ≠ latency. A fat pipe with a long path is great for downloads and terrible for conversation.
  • So the industry buys distance, not bandwidth. Caches, CDNs and edge locations exist to make d smaller. That is the whole idea behind Netflix appliances inside ISPs and AWS Edge Locations.

Despite the name, almost none of the internet is in the sky

Scale

~600 cable systems

Roughly 600 submarine cable systems were in service as of 2025 — 597 by the widely used count quoted in the slides.

Share of traffic

~99% by cable

Approximately 99% of intercontinental data traffic travels through these cables — not via satellite. Most people guess the opposite, and guess badly.

Physical form

Garden-hose thin

A cable carrying a significant share of a nation's traffic is often about as thick as a garden hose.

Why they break

The shark question (asked in class): Sharks biting cables is real — Google has armoured cables partly in response — but it is not the leading cause. The dominant causes are mundane and human: ship anchors and fishing gear. The slides put it bluntly: "the major cause of transatlantic network traffic issues are fishing anchors mistakenly damaging the cables."
The takeaway to write down: the internet feels weightless and abstract, and is in fact physically fragile in extremely boring ways.

Scenario B — it is 10 PM and Netflix is buffering

The answer is not "your WiFi is bad."

  • Netflix ships physical caching appliances into ISP networks so that the film starts a few kilometres away rather than on another continent.
  • When it stalls at peak hour, the bottleneck is usually congestion at the point where your ISP's network meets the content provider's — a peering point.
  • Who pays to upgrade that link is a commercial negotiation, not an engineering one. The Netflix–Comcast dispute of 2014 is the classic case.
The lesson: networks are not only wires and protocols. They are also economics, contracts and negotiation. That will keep surprising you.

A network you will never see but will depend on

If you intend to work in AI, this section matters more than any other in Lecture 1.

1. Your model does not fit in one GPU

Worked example — why a network is unavoidable 
// A 70-billion-parameter model at 2 bytes per parameter
70,000,000,000 params × 2 bytes = 140 GB of memory needed

An NVIDIA H100 has  80 GB

140 GB > 80 GB  → the model does NOT fit on one machine
                → it must be cut into pieces across machines
                → THE MOMENT YOU SPLIT IT, YOU HAVE A NETWORK

2. And the pieces must talk, constantly

At every training step, each GPU computes gradients for its slice and must combine them with every other GPU's result before anyone can take the next step. That operation is called an all-reduce.

Worked example — the rough calculation from class 
Link speed: 400 Gb/s  =  50 GB/s   (divide by 8)

Time to shift 140 GB   =  140 ÷ 50  =  ~3 seconds
Full exchange          ≈  5–6 seconds

Now multiply by HUNDREDS OF THOUSANDS of training steps.

If computation per step = 1 s and communication = 5 s,
you have built a very expensive space heater that is idle most of the time.

Real systems overlap communication with computation to hide part of this — which is itself a networking technique you will recognise later in the course.

3. The response: absurd amounts of bandwidth

  • A single AI server carries more network bandwidth than most university campuses — by a factor of hundreds.
  • A modern 72-GPU rack contains thousands of interconnect cables, on the order of two miles of copper inside a single cabinet.
  • The messy cable rooms of the 1990s were never solved; they were made expensive.

4. Three ideas to file away for later

Idea 1

This network may not lose packets

The public internet assumes packets are dropped and simply retransmits them. Here, one lost packet stalls the all-reduce, which stalls every GPU in the cluster. These fabrics use quite different flow control and congestion control.

Idea 2

The slowest link sets the speed of everything

An all-reduce finishes only when the last GPU reports in. One bad cable or one congested link slows a hundred thousand GPUs to its own pace. (Same shape as the bottleneck idea in Lecture 2.)

Idea 3

The OS is too slow to participate

At these speeds the network card writes directly into GPU memory, bypassing the OS entirely — this is RDMA. We spend this course building layers of abstraction; engineers throw them away when speed demands it. Understanding why they are allowed to is the real skill.

5. At scale, something is always broken

Meta published engineering details from training Llama 3 on 16,384 H100 GPUs: 419 unexpected interruptions over 54 days — roughly one every three hours. The overwhelming majority were hardware faults, with GPUs and HBM3 memory the single largest category.

A tale of two phone calls

The same ten-second silence, carried two different ways:

The landline callThe WhatsApp call

Name for thisCircuit switchingPacket switching

Circuit switching — the road not taken

The classic telephone model. Before a conversation could begin, the network reserved a dedicated end-to-end path and held it exclusively for the entire duration of the call. It is not a stupid design. It has one genuine virtue and one fatal flaw.

The virtue

Guaranteed capacity

Once the circuit is established, the full reserved capacity is yours: predictable, stable, no surprises mid-call. This is a real property — and one that packet switching does not have.

The flaw

Wasteful for bursty data

Go quiet for even a few seconds and that reserved capacity sits idle, unusable by anyone else, the whole time.

The word doing the work is "bursty". Voice is fairly steady, so circuit switching suited telephony reasonably well. Web browsing, messaging and file transfers are the opposite — long silences punctuated by short bursts. Reserving capacity for traffic like that means paying for a link you are barely using.
QUESTION ASKED IN CLASS. You are on a video call and go silent for 15 seconds. Under which technology is the network "wasting" capacity during your silence?

Answer: the landline — circuit switching. It reserved a dedicated path for the whole call, so your silence still holds that capacity. Under packet switching your silence sends zero packets, freeing the link for others. This is precisely why 10 Mbps of packet-switched bandwidth can serve far more users than 10 Mbps of reserved circuits.

What exactly is a packet?

Your message — a photo, a webpage, a voice clip — is chopped into small, independent chunks called packets. Three consequences follow, and all three matter later:

  • Each packet carries a header (addressing and control information) and a payload (your actual data).
  • Packets to the same destination can take different routes. They are independent — nothing binds them to a single path.
  • The core forwards each packet independently, hop by hop, router to router.

The store-and-forward rule

STORE-AND-FORWARD. A router must receive the entire packet before it sends even the first bit onward.
Receive fully → check → forward fully

Why it matters: no single router needs to know the whole path. It only needs to know the next hop. That is what makes a network of this size governable at all — no device anywhere holds a complete map of the internet.

The cost: every hop adds a full transmission delay before forwarding can begin.

Sharing on demand: statistical multiplexing

No reservations. Links are used only when packets actually need them. That single idea unlocks two distinct advantages — keep them separate in your head:

PropertyWhy packet switching gives it
EfficiencyReal traffic is bursty — you are rarely sending data every millisecond. Many users can share one link because it is statistically unlikely that they all burst at the same instant.
ResilienceBecause packets are not tied to one fixed path, traffic can be rerouted around a failed link or router. Nothing was reserved there, so nothing is lost when it disappears.

The name to remember: statistical multiplexing — sharing based on the statistics of how people actually behave, rather than on worst-case reservation.

The price: no guarantees (packet loss)

Packet switching buys efficiency and resilience by giving up certainty. Two things can happen to your packet in the core:

  1. It can be delayed, waiting in a queue behind other packets.
  2. It can be dropped entirely, when a router's buffer fills up under load. ← this is packet loss
THIS IS THE SEED OF EVERYTHING TCP DOES. In Weeks 5–6 we meet TCP — a protocol whose entire job is to paper over exactly this gap. It detects loss, retransmits what went missing, and paces data so that an unreliable, best-effort core feels reliable to your applications. Every mechanism in TCP exists because of a decision made in this lecture.

The toll-booth caravan (the analogy that fixes everything)

Picture a caravan of cars arriving at a toll booth, then driving down a long highway to the next toll booth. Each car must individually pass through the booth — one at a time, taking a fixed time each. Then everyone drives the highway together, at the speed limit.

Hold on to this mapping:
the toll booth is transmission delay  ·  the highway is propagation delay.
Almost every mistake students make on this topic comes from mixing those two up.

Every packet pays four kinds of delay

#DelayWhat it isSize / formula
1ProcessingRouter examines the header and checks for bit errorsTiny — microseconds
2QueuingTime spent waiting in a buffer behind other packetsVariable — we name it, we do not compute it
3TransmissionTime to push all of the packet's bits onto the linkL / R ← we compute this
4PropagationTime for a bit to physically travel along the linkd / s ← we compute this

Queuing delay is the awkward one. It is the only delay that depends on what everyone else is doing, which is why it is variable, why it causes jitter, and why it is the hardest to reason about. We name it here and return to it when we study congestion.

The two that get confused: transmission vs propagation

Transmission delay — "toll-booth time"Propagation delay — "highway drive time"
What it isPushing every bit through the booth, one after another, before anything can start down the highwayActually travelling the distance between the booths, at a fixed speed limit

SloganMore booths → fasterMore lanes ≠ faster
THE SENTENCE TO MEMORISE. A faster link reduces transmission delay. It does nothing whatsoever to propagation delay. You can buy more bandwidth; you cannot buy a shorter distance or a faster speed of light.

Deep dive — two threads worth pulling

Thread 1

Why big tech builds its own backbone

Google, Meta and Netflix lay their own private fibre between data centres and cities, shortening the physical path traffic must travel. Shorter d in d/s means lower propagation delay. They are not buying bandwidth here — they are buying distance.

Thread 2

Why packet size isn't "bigger is better"

Huge packets: long waits at each hop before forwarding can start (store-and-forward). Tiny packets: more headers and per-packet processing overhead, so more of the link carries bookkeeping than data. There is an optimum in the middle; real networks settle it with a maximum transmission unit (MTU).

Exam scope reminder: only transmission (L/R) and propagation (d/s) are computed. Queuing-delay maths is explicitly out of scope.

Transmission delay — pushing bits onto the link

Transmission delay = L / R

L = packet length in bits · R = link rate in bits per second

Worked example 1 — from the lecture 
Given: L = 8,000 bits,  R = 2 Mbps

Step 1 — convert units:   2 Mbps = 2,000,000 bits/second
Step 2 — divide:          8,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 0.004 s
Step 3 — express in ms:   0.004 × 1000 = 4 ms

Why doubling the link rate matters

Link rate RTransmission delay for an 8,000-bit packet
2 Mbps4 ms
4 Mbps2 ms
8 Mbps1 ms

Transmission delay is inversely proportional to link rate. Doubling the rate halves the delay, every time. This is the part of latency that money can genuinely buy.

Propagation delay — the bit's physical journey

Propagation delay = d / s

d = distance in metres · s = signal speed ≈ 2 × 10⁸ m/s in optical fibre (≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s in free space, e.g. a satellite radio link)

Worked example 2 — India to the UK 
Fibre cable route ≈ 9,000 km (much longer than the straight-line distance)

Step 1 — convert:   9,000 km = 9,000,000 m
Step 2 — divide:    9,000,000 ÷ (2 × 10⁸) = 0.045 s
Step 3 — in ms:     = 45 ms one way
Step 4 — round trip: ≈ 90 ms

…before a single router, queue or server has done anything at all.
No upgrade to your broadband plan will reduce it.

Case study — the 500 Mbps link that still feels slow

A geostationary satellite link is advertised at a generous 500 Mbps. Yet a video call over it still has that awkward lag where both people keep talking over each other. Why?

The satellite orbits about 35,786 km above the equator. The signal must climb up and come back down — roughly 71,500 km — before it has travelled one way.

JourneyDistanceTime at 3 × 10⁸ m/s
Up to the satellite and back down (one way, A → B)~71,500 km~240 ms
A full round trip (A → B → A)~143,000 km~480 ms
A PRECISION WORTH HAVING. ~240 ms is the one-way propagation delay. What you actually feel in a conversation is the round-trip time — you speak, the sound reaches them, they react, and their reply comes back. That is roughly 480 ms, which is where the "awkward half-second lag" genuinely comes from.

Be careful with "round trip" in this course: it means source → destination → source, not simply up and down.
The moral: bandwidth and latency are independent. A 500 Mbps link with 480 ms round-trip delay is excellent for downloading a large file and unusable for a conversation. When someone says a connection is "fast", always ask which one they mean.

Two definitions

Definition

Throughput

The rate, in bits per second, at which data is actually received. Not advertised, not theoretical — what lands.

Definition

Bottleneck link

End-to-end throughput ≈ the minimum link rate anywhere on the path. The slowest hop sets the pace for everyone.

The letter analogy

You write a letter, put it in an envelope, and hand it to the postal service. Does the mail carrier care about the letter's contents? No.

And that "no" is the whole idea. The carrier needs the address on the outside and nothing else. You need to know how to write a letter and nothing about sorting offices, vans or flight routes. Neither of you needs to understand the other's job — and the letter still arrives.

AND WHAT IF THEY DO CARE? (asked in class) If the carrier reads the letter, the separation breaks: your privacy is gone, and the carrier now has to understand every kind of message anybody might send. A layer that looks inside the layer above it stops being a clean layer.

This is the seed of two later topics: encryption (Lecture 6 — making contents unreadable to the carrier) and the question of which devices are allowed to look how deep into your traffic (Lecture 4 — firewalls, packet filtering, deep packet inspection).

What layering buys us

  • Each layer solves one problem, and can be understood on its own.
  • A layer can be replaced without disturbing the others — swap WiFi for Ethernet and nothing above needs rewriting.
  • Different vendors can build different layers and still interoperate, because the boundary between layers is agreed in advance.
  • When something breaks, you can localise the fault to a layer instead of searching the whole system.

Protocols vs services — the distinction the syllabus asks for

Horizontal

Protocol

The agreed rules and message formats between peer layers on two different machines. Layer 3 on your laptop speaks to Layer 3 on the server — that agreement is a protocol (IP, TCP, HTTP, TLS).

Vertical

Service

What a layer offers to the layer directly above it on the same machine. Layer 3 offers "get this packet to that address" to Layer 4; Layer 4 offers "reliable byte stream" to Layer 7.

Memory hook: protocols are horizontal (across the network, peer to peer). Services are vertical (up and down your own stack).

The nesting doll

Encapsulation works like a set of nesting dolls — the toy shown in class.

  • Going down the sender's stack: each layer wraps the data handed to it from above with its own header.
  • Coming up the receiver's stack: decapsulation reverses the process — each layer strips off its own specific header and hands the remaining payload upward.
The important property is symmetry. Layer 3 on the receiving machine reads exactly the header that Layer 3 on the sending machine wrote, and neither of them touches anything belonging to another layer. Each layer is, in effect, having a private conversation with its counterpart at the other end.

The gift-wrapping version

The same idea, told the way it was framed in class for the OSI model: you wrap a gift in layers — paper, then a box, then ribbon — going out, and you unwrap them in exactly the reverse order when the gift is received. You cannot untie the ribbon last.

Learn the numbers as well as the names. In practice people say "Layer 3" far more often than "Network layer".

Layer 7 — Application

What it does. Delivers network services directly to end users and supports applications such as web browsers, email and file transfer. Its unit of concern is simply data.

The analogy — a waiter in a restaurant. It takes your order (the user request), relays it to the kitchen (the network), and comes back with your food (the data). The customer never enters the kitchen; the chef never speaks to the customer. The waiter's whole job is connecting the user to the service efficiently.

Layer 6 — Presentation

What it does. Formats, encrypts and compresses data so it is readable and secure for the receiving application. Two headline responsibilities: translation and encryption.

Concrete jobs that live here:

  • ASCII text → Unicode, and other character-encoding conversions
  • Compression / decompression — zip and unzip
  • Encryption / decryption
  • Encoding images according to type: PNG, JPEG, GIF

The analogy — a translator who converts, formats and secures messages so sender and receiver understand each other even when they speak different languages. From the slides: the data has to be presented the way the application wants it, and the way your system wants it — those are not always the same thing.

Layer 5 — Session

What it does. Establishes, manages and terminates communication sessions between two devices. Two named responsibilities: dialog control and synchronization.

The analogy — a courtroom, the judge calling "Order! Order!" and keeping proceedings continuous. Or a panel moderator: it starts the call, keeps the connection running smoothly, and ends it when everyone is finished. Somebody has to decide whose turn it is and when the conversation is over.

Layer 4 — Transport

What it does. Ensures reliable and efficient delivery of data between two devices across a network. A message is encapsulated with detailed source and destination addressing, broken up for transit, and reassembled at the receiver.

The analogy — many separate conversations sharing one phone line. Multiplexing is what ensures each message reaches the right recipient rather than the wrong one.

THE LINK BACK TO LECTURE 2. This is where TCP lives — the protocol whose job is to paper over the fact that the core offers no guarantees. Loss detection, retransmission and pacing are all Layer 4 concerns.

Layer 3 — Network

What it does. Responsible for logical addressing and for forwarding data packets across different networks. Stated plainly: get packets from point A to point B.

The analogy — a GPS system, finding the best route for delivery trucks (the packets) to reach their destination. It handles addressing and routing, much as you would choose the best roads to arrive fastest and most safely.

Note the word "different" in "across different networks". Layer 3 is the layer that stops caring whether the next network is Ethernet, WiFi or fibre. That indifference is what allows a network of networks to exist at all.

Layer 2 — Data Link

What it does. Ensures reliable data transfer between two directly connected nodes, and handles error detection and MAC addressing. Its scope is deliberately narrow: one hop, done correctly.

Two responsibilities named in class:

  • Media access control — "one at a time!" When several devices share a medium, something must decide who transmits when, or they will talk over each other.
  • Error checking — detecting that the bits which arrived are the bits that were sent.
THE MOST-CONFUSED DISTINCTION. Layer 3 worries about the whole journey; Layer 2 worries only about getting safely to the other end of one link.

Layer 1 — Physical

What it does. Transmits raw bits — zeros and ones — over a physical medium.

The media discussed in class: coaxial cable · fibre optic cable · radio waves.

The analogy — the roads, wires and signals the postman actually uses to move around. No addresses, no routes, no meaning — just the physical means of getting something from here to there.

This is also the layer where Lecture 2's propagation delay lives. The speed of light in fibre is a Layer 1 fact, and no amount of clever protocol design at any higher layer can argue with it.

The TCP/IP model

A layered framework that defines how data is transmitted over the internet, using standard protocols such as TCP and IP.

The important thing to understand is its status. OSI is the model we use to think and to teach. TCP/IP is the model the internet actually runs on. They are not competitors so much as two different kinds of object — one a clean conceptual scheme, the other a working system that grew.

The idea that fixes it

THE PIVOT. Instead of connecting everyone to everyone — what if everyone connects to ONE device?

The hub — the 1980s answer

  • Function: broadcasts incoming data to all connected devices.
  • It does not recognise hardware addresses (MAC addresses) at all.
  • Operates at the Physical layer — Layer 1.

A hub is the most literal possible interpretation of "connect everyone to one device". It takes the electrical signal arriving on one port and repeats it out of every other port. It has no idea what a device is, let alone which one you meant.

THE QUESTION ASKED IN CLASS. What is the main real-world problem with using a hub in a network with many devices?

Answer: unnecessary traffic. Data is broadcast to every device even when only one device needs it. Every machine has to receive, inspect and discard traffic that was never meant for it — and while that traffic occupies the shared medium, nobody else can transmit. The more devices you add, the worse it gets.

The switch — the 1990s answer

  • Operates at the Data Link layer — Layer 2.
  • Connects devices within a LAN and forwards data to the correct device using MAC addresses.
  • It has no understanding of Layer 3 — IP addresses mean nothing to it.
  • Ports can be configured to control how connections are routed.
  • Broadcasting can be controlled, because the switch has MAC-level intelligence about which device sits on which port.

The switch is the hub with a memory. Because it learns which MAC address lives behind which port (the MAC/CAM table), it can send a frame only where it needs to go. The unnecessary-traffic problem largely disappears.

What a topology is

Network topology describes the arrangement of devices and their connections within a network, including how data flows through it. The types named in class: star, mesh, hybrid, ring and bus.

Star topology

  • All devices connect to a central hub or switch.
  • Data flows from the sending device to the hub/switch, which then forwards it to the intended receiver.
  • The centralised structure simplifies diagnostics and maintenance — there is one obvious place to look.
  • A popular choice for LANs, because the design stays manageable as it grows.

This is the direct descendant of "everyone connects to one device". Its weakness is the mirror image of its strength — that central device is a single point of failure.

Mesh topology

  • Every network device is directly connected to all other devices.
  • This creates many possible paths for sending data between any two devices.
  • Reliability increases: if one path fails, data can take another route.

You already met the cost: the n(n−1)/2 problem. Mesh topologies are jokingly called spaghetti networks for exactly that reason. The idea remains valuable where the links are not physical cables: wireless mesh networks are widely used to extend WiFi coverage, because adding a node adds paths rather than cabling.

Hybrid topology

Combines two or more different topologies to form a more complex and flexible network — for example star, ring and bus segments joined together. Real networks of any size are almost always hybrids, because different parts of an organisation have different needs.

The online shopping delivery problem (the analogy for the whole tab)

Imagine you order an outfit online: a Shirt, Pants, Shoes and Socks — these are your HTML, CSS, JS and Images, all requested from the server. How would you prefer them delivered? Your preference is the answer to the "why" of HTTP versions.

HTTP/1.0 (1996) — one item per trip

The truck can only carry one item per trip, and must return to HQ before delivering the next. A massive waste of time.

  • Carries one request at a time.
  • Every file (HTML, image, CSS) = a new connection.
  • No persistent TCP connection.
  • Slow, repetitive and inefficient.

"I deliver one file at a time. It can get tiring!"

HTTP/1.1 (1997) — the one-truck convoy

The truck arrives at your house with all 4 items — but unloads them one by one.

  • Introduced Keep-Alive (persistent) connections.
  • Introduced caching and Host headers.
  • Improved performance but had head-of-line blocking.

Head-of-Line (HoL) blocking

ONE CONNECTION · responses must exit in order (FIFO).

A slow image at the head of the queue blocks the CSS, JS and icon behind it — even though they are already ready. That is head-of-line blocking.

HTTP/1.1 pipelining (optional):

  • Responses must arrive in the same order as requests (FIFO — First In, First Out).
  • Only safe methods can be pipelined: GET, HEAD, OPTIONS.
  • No POST/PUT — these might have side effects, as they alter the state of the destination.
  • "Multiple requests can be made without waiting for the response to the previous request."

HTTP/2 (2015) — serve the whole tray

Instead of serving your meal one by one, I will serve you the entire tray and you choose what to eat.

HTTP/2 is the second major version of HTTP, aimed at enhancing data transfer performance between clients and servers. It improves on HTTP/1.1 by addressing its performance limitations.

Feature 1

Binary protocol

Uses a binary format for message encoding, unlike text-based HTTP/1.x. This is the framing layer.

Feature 2

Multiplexing

Simultaneous requests and responses over ONE connection — no more 6 parallel TCP connections faking parallelism.

Feature 3

Server Push

The server can send resources to the client proactively, without explicit requests.

The catch HTTP/2 does NOT fix. HTTP/2 fixes application-level HoL blocking. But it still runs over TCP — and one lost TCP segment stalls ALL streams on that connection, because TCP guarantees in-order delivery of the whole byte stream. That is TCP-level HoL blocking, and it remains.

HTTP/3 using QUIC (June 2022) — dedicated kitchen windows

"You order 3 items at a restaurant and their entire staff starts serving only you — everything you ordered has its own separate kitchen window and dedicated staff."

  • Latest version of the HTTP protocol.
  • Built on QUIC, a UDP-based transport protocol instead of TCP, developed by Google.
  • Multiplexed streams without blocking.
  • Designed to solve head-of-line blocking.

Why REST exists

We understand the client–server model, where a browser requests a page from a server. But what happens when an application needs to communicate with another application? That is what REST APIs are for.

A Q4 2024 analysis estimated that 83% of all public APIs use REST architecture (figure quoted in the slides).

REST principles

Principle

Client–server separation

The client and server evolve independently; they only agree on the interface.

Principle

Every request contains everything the server needs. The server stores no client session state between requests — exactly like HTTP itself.

Principle

Resources are identified by URLs, and acted on with the standard HTTP methods. Same rules for every resource.

Responses declare whether they may be cached, so repeated reads need not hit the server.

A client cannot tell whether it is talking to the origin server or to a proxy / load balancer in between. (Lecture 3's layering, applied at Layer 7.)

A machine-readable description of an API so that documentation, clients and tests can be generated from one source. Demoed in class via petstore3.swagger.io.

What is cryptography?

The practice of securing information by converting readable text into unreadable code (ciphertext) and back again.

The three core goals of cryptography

Goal 1

Confidentiality (Privacy)

Ensures that information is accessible only to authorized parties.
Slogan: "No eavesdropping."

Goal 2

Integrity

Guarantees that data remains accurate and has not been altered during transit or storage.
Slogan: "No tampering."

Goal 3

Authentication

Verifies the identities of both the sender and the receiver.
Slogan: "Verified identity."

Memory hook — C.I.A. Confidentiality · Integrity · Authentication. Map each to its slogan and you have three exam marks: no eavesdropping · no tampering · verified identity.

How TLS delivers each goal

GoalDelivered by
ConfidentialitySymmetric encryption of the session using the shared session key
IntegrityMessage authentication / integrity checks on every record
AuthenticationThe server's digital certificate, signed by a Certificate Authority

Encryption and decryption

Plaintext → (encrypt with a key) → ciphertext → (decrypt with a key) → plaintext.

Symmetric encryption

One single shared key is used for both encryption and decryption. Both sides must already have the same key.

ANALYSE: what's the issue with using symmetric encryption? (asked in class)

How do you share the key securely? To send the key over the network you would need a secure channel — but establishing a secure channel is exactly the problem you were trying to solve. This is the key-distribution problem, and it is why asymmetric encryption exists.

Asymmetric encryption

Uses a key pair: a public key (published to anyone) and a private key (never leaves the owner).

  • Anything encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the matching private key → gives confidentiality.
  • Anything signed with the private key can be verified by anyone holding the public key → gives authentication. This is how certificate signatures work.

SMTP — Simple Mail Transfer Protocol

  • Sends email from sender server to recipient servers.
  • Push-based protocol.
  • Uses TCP. Ports: 25 / 587 / 465.

How SMTP works: the email client (Gmail app, Outlook) connects to the SMTP server → SMTP pushes the message from the sender to the receiver's mail server → the receiver then uses POP3 or IMAP to retrieve it.

SMTP in the real world: SendGrid, Mailchimp and Mailgun integrations use SMTP relays. MailHog was the local testing tool named in class.

IMAP — Internet Message Access Protocol

  • Manages email directly on the server — messages stay remote.
  • Suitable for multi-device access.
  • Supports folders and partial downloads.
  • Port 143; secure (IMAPS): 993.
  • Connection type: two-way sync (client ↔ server).

How IMAP works: your client connects to the server → you view and manage emails without downloading them completely → changes (read/unread, folders, deletions) are reflected on the server and synced across all devices → you can access folders (Inbox, Sent, Drafts) as organised on the server.

POP3 — Post Office Protocol v3

  • Downloads email to the local client and deletes it from the server.
  • Best for offline access, but poor sync across devices.
  • Port 110; secure (POP3S): 995.
  • Connection type: one-way (server → client).

How POP3 works: client connects → downloads the emails → deletes them from the server → emails now exist on your local device only.

The problem, and the one-word answer

Imagine: the same content requested repeatedly. Solution? Caching.

Definition (learn this wording): caching is "the process of storing copies of data in a temporary, high-speed storage layer so that future requests for that same data can be served much faster than fetching it from the original, slower source."
Outcome 1

Cache Hit

The requested data is in the cache. Served immediately from the fast local layer — no network trip to the origin.

Outcome 2

Cache Miss

The data is not in the cache. Must be fetched from the original, slower source — and is then typically stored for next time.

How caching works, step by step

  1. A client requests a resource (image, CSS file).
  2. The server responds and may include cache-control headers.
  3. The client stores the resource in its local cache.
  4. On the next request, the client checks: is the cached copy still valid (not expired)?
    • If valid → use from cache (no network trip).
    • If expired → revalidate or fetch again.
The Cache-Control header format from the slides 
Cache-Control: public, max-age=3600

// public  → may be stored by any cache (browser, proxy, CDN)
// max-age → how many SECONDS the copy stays fresh (3600 = 1 hour)

Caching behaviour = how data is stored temporarily and retrieved to improve performance and reduce latency. Used in networking, browsers, web servers, CDNs and applications.

The grocery-store analogy from class

A CDN is like a chain of grocery stores. Instead of travelling to faraway farms, shoppers visit a local store. Local stores stock food from distant farms, saving time and effort. Similarly, CDNs cache web content closer to users. Result: webpages load faster — just as shopping takes minutes, not days.

Definition

A CDN is a set of geographically distributed servers that delivers content from the nearest edge server, reduces latency and saves bandwidth.

How a CDN works

  1. A user requests content (a video or image) from a website.
  2. The nearest edge server serves the request instead of the origin server.
  3. If not cached, the edge server retrieves it from the origin and caches it.
  4. Benefits: faster load times, reduced bandwidth usage, improved availability.

Speed vs reliability — the UDP trade-off

The cartoon from the slides says it best. Sender: "Are you getting all of this?"  ·  UDP: "Who cares, just send it faster!"

Where UDP shines — the four use cases named in class

Use case 1

Online gaming

Real-time speed matters more than perfect accuracy — it is better to lose a few packets than to experience delays, because late data is often useless.

Use case 2

Live video streaming

Unlike buffered YouTube, live streams prioritise speed. UDP sends video packets without delays; if some frames are lost they are skipped to avoid lag, keeping the flow smooth and instant.

Use case 3

SNMP & TFTP

SNMP sends small, quick status requests (is this router online?) and needs no connection — if a reply is lost it simply retries. TFTP transfers small config/boot files in trusted local networks, where speed and simplicity beat reliability.

Use case 4

DHCP

Quickly assigns IP addresses when a device joins a network. One-time requests and responses, relying on the application to handle any needed reliability.

DNS belongs on this list too (Lecture 8 / Lab 3): port 53, UDP by default because queries are small and a lost query is simply re-sent.

QUIC — Quick UDP Internet Connections

  • A modern transport protocol built on top of UDP, designed by Google.
  • Now used in HTTP/3 to make browsing faster and more secure by combining UDP's speed with TCP-like reliability.
  • Unlike TCP, QUIC avoids slow handshakes, efficiently handles packet loss, and offers built-in TLS encryption, faster connection setup and stream multiplexing.
  • Used by Google, YouTube and Facebook.
Tie this back to Lecture 5: QUIC is why HTTP/3 solves TCP-level head-of-line blocking — streams over UDP are genuinely independent, so one lost packet stalls only its own stream.

The TCP 3-way handshake — told as the class story

sequenceDiagram
  participant C as Your browser (client)
  participant S as Google server (port 443)
  C->>S: 1. SYN — "I'd like to talk. My sentences start at 100."
  S->>C: 2. SYN-ACK — "I'm here. I ACK your 100. Mine start at 350."

StepPacketWhat it says
1. The Ring SYN (synchronize)"Hey, are you there? I'd like to start a conversation. To keep our chat organised, I'll start numbering my sentences at 100."
2. The Hello SYN-ACK"Yes, I'm here! I ACKnowledge your request to start at 100. And to keep my side organised, I'll SYNchronize and start at 350. Are you ready?"
3. The I'm Here ACK"Got it! I ACKnowledge that you're starting at 350. Let's talk!" → ESTABLISHED
Why it takes three and not two: both sides must announce and have confirmed their own starting sequence number. The server's SYN-ACK does two jobs at once (ACK the client's SYN, and SYN its own), which is why the total is three and not four.

TCP connection teardown — the 4-way handshake

sequenceDiagram
  participant C as Client
  participant S as Server
  C->>S: 1. FIN — "I have to go."
  S->>C: 2. ACK — "Okay."
  Note over S: server finishes sending remaining data
  S->>C: 3. FIN — "I'm done too."
  C->>S: 4. ACK — "Bye."
Setup is 3 steps; teardown is 4. The reason: on teardown the server cannot combine its ACK and its FIN, because it may still have data left to send after acknowledging the client's FIN. On setup there is nothing to finish, so SYN-ACK can be combined.

Sequence numbers — ordered delivery

Messages can take different routes and arrive out of order. Imagine reading a story that arrived as: "the mat." "The cat" "sat on". TCP solves this with the sequence numbers established during the handshake. Every segment is numbered; if the receiver gets #3 before #2, it waits for #2 before passing the story up to the application in the correct order.

Acknowledgments and retransmission

How do you know the other person heard you on a phone call? You listen for them to say "hmm" or "achcha."

  • When the server receives a chunk (say segments 101–200), it sends an ACK saying "I've received everything up to 201" — i.e. the ACK number is the next expected byte.
  • If the sender doesn't get an ACK within a certain time, it assumes the data was lost and retransmits it.

What TCP provides, in four lines

  • Establishes a connection prior to data transmission.
  • Maintains data integrity using sequence numbers, acknowledgments and checksums.
  • Ensures reliable, ordered delivery of data packets, handling any loss.
  • Handles the connection teardown process as well.
Teardown recap (from Lecture 9): FIN → ACK → FIN → ACK, a 4-way handshake.

Why a checksum exists

As TCP aims to provide reliable data transfer, the checksum detects transmission errors so that corrupted data can be retransmitted.

The worked example from class — reproduce this exactly

Alice sends two 8-bit chunks to Bob 
Chunk 1: 10010011   (147 decimal)
Chunk 2: 01010110   (86 decimal)

STEP 1 — Sender adds the two chunks together.
   10010011
 + 01010110
 ───────────
  1 11101001        ← a carry-out of 1

STEP 2 — Take that carry-over 1 and add it back to the result
          (this is "wrap-around carry" / one's-complement addition).
   11101001
 +        1
 ───────────
   11101010

STEP 3 — Perform a 1's complement: FLIP ALL THE BITS.
   11101010  →  00010101

So the checksum is the flipped value. The slides quote the
checksum for this example as 11110101.

What Bob does

  1. Alice sends the original data chunks AND the checksum.
  2. Bob performs the same calculation on the data he received.
  3. Bob then adds the checksum he received.
  4. If the final result is all 1s, TCP knows with high probability that the data is perfect
The three steps to write in an exam: ① add the chunks (with wrap-around carry) ② add the carry back in ③ take the 1's complement (flip every bit). Verification = sum everything including the checksum; all 1s means no error detected.

The waiting game — Round Trip Time

Alice needs a receipt (an ACK) to know her data arrived safely. How long should she wait before assuming it was lost? Ten milliseconds? A full second? That is what RTT answers.

RTT = the total time for a segment to go from sender to receiver AND for the receiver's acknowledgment to come back.

Measure it with: ping newtonschool.co  ·  ping taobao.com — the second will be much larger, which is Lecture 2's d/s showing up in a real tool.

Stop-and-Wait

sequenceDiagram
  participant S as Sender
  participant R as Receiver
  S->>R: Packet N
  Note over S: STOPS. Waits. Sends nothing.
  R->>R: receives N, processes it
  R->>S: ACK (N+1) — "got N, send me N+1"
  S->>R: Packet N+1
  1. Sender transmits a data packet with sequence number N.
  2. Sender stops all transmission and waits.
  3. Receiver receives packet N and processes it.
  4. Receiver sends ACK with acknowledgment number N+1 — "I received N, send me N+1 next".
Why stop-and-wait is bad: very inefficient for high-latency or high-bandwidth networks. On a long-distance link the sender wastes time waiting for each ACK before sending again. Utilisation is low, because transmission time is tiny compared with propagation delay.

This is the problem the sliding window exists to solve.

Why flow control is needed

  • Not all devices have the same buffer size or processing power.
  • A fast sender could flood a slow receiver, leading to packet loss or buffer overflow.
  • TCP flow control prevents this by letting the receiver say: "Hey, slow down — I can only handle this much right now."
Flow control ≠ congestion control. Flow control protects the RECEIVER (one slow machine). Congestion control protects the NETWORK (everyone else) — that is Lecture 11. This distinction is a guaranteed exam question.

The sliding window protocol

  • TCP uses a sliding window protocol to manage flow control.
  • The receiver tells the sender how much data it can receive at a time — the window size (carried in the TCP header's Window Size field).
  • As the receiver processes data and acknowledges packets, the sender "slides" the window forward and sends more.

Zero Window condition

A Zero Window occurs when the receiver's buffer is full and it temporarily tells the sender to stop sending data. This is done by the receiver advertising a window size of zero in the TCP header.

The sender then pauses and periodically probes until the receiver advertises a non-zero window again.

Go-Back-N (GBN)

An error control protocol for reliable communication over noisy channels. It lets the sender send multiple frames before receiving an acknowledgment — but if a frame is lost or erroneous, the sender must retransmit that frame AND all subsequent frames.

  • Sender sends N frames at once without waiting for individual ACKs.
  • Receiver only accepts frames in order (sequentially).
  • If a frame is lost or corrupted, the receiver discards that frame and all that follow — even if they are correct.
  • The sender goes back to the last acknowledged frame and retransmits everything from there.

Selective Repeat (SR)

Also an error-control protocol for reliable delivery over unreliable networks. Unlike Go-Back-N, it only retransmits the frames that were lost or corrupted, not all subsequent frames.

  • Sender can send multiple frames up to a window size without waiting for ACKs.
  • Receiver can accept and buffer frames out of order.
  • If a frame is lost or corrupted: the receiver discards only that frame and sends a NACK (or simply doesn't ACK it).
  • The sender retransmits only the missing frame, not the whole window.

Congestion window (cwnd)

cwnd = a sender-side limit on the amount of data the sender can have in flight before receiving an acknowledgment from the client.

For new connections it is 10 by default in most operating systems.
Receive window (rwnd)Congestion window (cwnd)
Set byThe receiverThe sender, by inference
ProtectsThe receiver's bufferThe network
Lecture10 — flow control11 — congestion control

The actual amount a sender may transmit is limited by whichever of the two is smaller.

Two special cases of TCP timeouts (named in class)

  • Lost ACK scenario — the data arrived, but the acknowledgment was lost, so the sender retransmits unnecessarily.
  • Premature timeout — the timer expired too early; the ACK was merely slow, not lost.

MSS — Maximum Segment Size

MSS is the largest amount of data, in bytes, that a device can send in the payload of a single TCP segment. Congestion window sizes are counted in MSS units.

Slow Start — the cars picture

1 → send just a single car on the road · 2 → next time send two cars · 4 → then send four cars. Doubling every RTT.
  • When the connection is established, it starts slowly in a phase called Slow Start.
  • Initially it sends a small number of segments.
  • If those packets are acknowledged, it DOUBLES the number sent.
  • This doubling helps to find an optimal number of segments to send.
The name is misleading — a classic exam trap. "Slow Start" refers to starting from a small window, not to growing slowly. The growth is exponential (doubling each RTT) — it is the fastest growth phase TCP has.

When does Slow Start end?

Slow Start continues until either:

  1. The congestion window reaches a threshold called the slow start threshold (ssthresh), where
    ssthresh = cwnd / 2
    — half the value of the congestion window at the moment congestion was detected. It then switches to Congestion Avoidance: instead of doubling, it adds just one extra packet at a time — a gentle, linear increase.
  2. OR packet loss occurs — detected by a timeout or three duplicate ACKs.

AIMD — Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease

The approach: senders can increase sending rate until packet loss (congestion) occurs, then decrease sending rate on a loss event.
The "AI"

Additive Increase

Every RTT with no packet loss: increase cwnd linearly by 1 MSS.

The "MD"

Multiplicative Decrease

· Cut in HALF on loss detected by three duplicate ACKs.
· Cut to 1 MSS when loss is detected by timeout.

The distinction worth 2 marks: 3 duplicate ACKs → halve cwnd (the network is congested but still delivering — a mild signal). Timeout → cwnd = 1 MSS (nothing is getting through at all — a severe signal). The punishment matches the severity.

The TCP Sawtooth pattern

AIMD operates by having the sender increase its transmission rate (additive increase) to probe for bandwidth. Upon packet loss, indicating congestion, it halves the rate (multiplicative decrease), creating a sawtooth wave pattern.

TCP CUBIC

Is there a better way than AIMD to "probe" for usable bandwidth?

  • CUBIC increases W as a function of the cube of the distance between the current time and the time when the TCP window size will reach Wmax.
  • Wmax = the sending rate at which congestion loss was detected.
  • TCP CUBIC is the default in Linux, and the most popular TCP for web servers.

Why Fast Retransmit and Fast Recovery are needed

TCP detects packet loss either through a timeout (slow and inefficient) or multiple duplicate ACKs (faster mechanism). To speed up recovery and avoid unnecessary delays, TCP uses both mechanisms below.

Fast Retransmit

  • If the sender receives three duplicate ACKs (e.g. ACK 5, ACK 5, ACK 5)…
  • …it assumes a packet was lost, likely the next in sequence,
  • and it immediately retransmits that missing packet.

Faster than waiting for a timer to expire.

Fast Recovery

  • Used immediately after Fast Retransmit.
  • Instead of going back to Slow Start, TCP enters Congestion Avoidance directly, allowing smoother performance.
The logic to state in an answer: three duplicate ACKs prove packets are still arriving — the pipe is not dead, just congested. Collapsing to Slow Start would over-punish. A timeout, by contrast, means nothing is getting through, which justifies the full reset.

What a load balancer is

A load balancer distributes incoming network traffic across multiple servers to ensure high availability, reliability, scalability and optimal application performance.

It makes its decision on the basis of two approaches: Network Load Balancer and Application Load Balancer.

Network Load Balancer (NLB) — Layer 4

Recap: the transport tuple. A TCP/UDP connection is identified by a 4-tuple: Source IP, Source Port, Destination IP, Destination Port.
  • NLB operates at the Transport Layer (Layer 4).
  • It sees TCP/UDP packets — not HTTP requests, not URLs, not headers.
  • Analogy: a postal sorter who reads only the ZIP code, not the letter inside.

Benefits: ultra-high throughput · very low latency · preserves client source IP · excellent for TCP/UDP workloads.

Limitation — the question asked in class. Suppose we have company.com/api/users and company.com/images/logo.png. Can NLB distinguish them? No — because NLB cannot read the URL.

Application Load Balancer (ALB) — Layer 7

  • ALB operates at the Application Layer (Layer 7).
  • ALB understands: HTTP requests · URLs · headers · cookies · host names.

Elasticity

Elasticity means: increase capacity when demand rises, and decrease capacity when demand falls.

Auto Scaling Group

An Auto Scaling Group automatically:

  • Launches instances
  • Terminates instances
  • Maintains desired capacity

Scaling out — when traffic increases suddenly, the group goes from (before) EC2-1 to (after) EC2-1, EC2-2, EC2-3.

Vocabulary check: scaling OUT = adding more instances (horizontal). Scaling up would mean a bigger instance (vertical). An Auto Scaling Group does the former.

The problem — data centre failure

Suppose all servers are in us-east-1a. Everything works until a power failure, network failure or storage failure — and then the entire application is down.

Availability Zone

An Availability Zone (AZ) is a physically separate AWS data center. Example: us-east-1a, us-east-1b, us-east-1c. Each AZ is isolated.

The answer is Multi-Availability Zone deployment.

High Availability

High Availability means: the service remains available even when components fail.

Principle: NO SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE.
Examples of single points of failure: one server · one AZ · one database

The Great Address Shortage

  • The internet grew much faster than anyone predicted.
  • A company needing 500 addresses couldn't get a Class C (254 — too small) and had to take a Class B (over 65,000 addresses), wasting the rest.
  • We were running out of available IPv4 addresses.
The classes had a significant amount of difference in available IP addresses for hosts. This meant that, practically, a lot of IPv4 addresses remained unassigned.
The 2025 price, quoted in class: purchasing a public IPv4 address costs roughly $20–$45 per address, while leasing can be done for about $0.40 per IP per month.

Why subnet at all

One large network becomes difficult to manage. Subnetting means splitting one big network into smaller networks.

Borrowing host bits

A CIDR block has two parts: Network bits + Host bits. We subnet by borrowing bits from the host part and giving them to the network part.

Example: 10.0.0.0/24 → /24 means 24 network bits → 32 − 24 = 8 host bits.

The method, exactly as worked in the class notes

Problem: we need 4 subnets out of a /24 
STEP 1 — how many host bits do we start with?
32 − 24 = 8 host bits  →  2^8 = 256 addresses

STEP 2 — how many bits must we borrow?
2^k subnets, so we need 2^k ≥ 4
2^2 = 4  →  k = 2

STEP 3 — new prefix
/24 + 2 = /26

STEP 4 — how big is each subnet?
host bits left = 8 − 2 = 6
each /26 gets 2^6 = 64 addresses

STEP 5 — sanity check
4 subnets × 64 = 256  ✓  (matches the original /24)
Why borrowing 2 bits gives exactly 4 subnets: the 2 borrowed bits can take four combinations — 00, 01, 10, 11 — so they name four separate subnets. Each then has 6 host bits left → 2⁶ = 64 total addresses.

Doing 2^k maths is slow. Use the magic number instead.

The problem

Private subnets (e.g. 192.168.0.0/16) cannot be reached from the Internet, yet a phone on home WiFi with a private IP loads every site. The router at the border does the trick.

NAT — rewrite addresses at the border

  • Outbound: router swaps the private source IP for a public one (192.168.1.20 → 203.0.113.7).
  • Inbound reply: it reverses the swap — possible only because the router remembers the mapping in a translation table.

PAT — Port Address Translation

  • Adds the port number to the translation, so one public IP serves many hosts at once (what your home router does every second).
  • Table: 192.168.1.20:51000 → 203.0.113.7:40001, 192.168.1.21:51000 → 203.0.113.7:40002.
  • Two hosts can reuse inside port 51000 — the outside port is the key that routes each reply home. Every mapping must be unique.

Gain vs break

Gain: conserves scarce public IPv4; hides internal hosts (a security side-effect). Break: end-to-end reachability; inbound traffic now needs explicit setup.

Who configures every new device?

Nobody types IP settings by hand. One DHCP lease delivers four things: IP address, subnet mask, default gateway, DNS server. Leases expire, so addresses are renewed and recycled.

DORA — the four-step handshake

  • Discover — client broadcasts “anyone out there?” (src 0.0.0.0 → 255.255.255.255)
  • Offer — server proposes an address
  • Request — client asks for that one (also broadcast)
  • Ack — server confirms the lease
Memory hook: Discover and Request are broadcasts — the whole subnet hears them. The address handed out comes from the subnet range (Lecture 14).

What a route table is

  • A list of destination prefix → next hop, checked for every packet.
  • Longest-prefix match: the most specific route wins. For 10.0.1.55 with 0.0.0.0/0, 10.0.0.0/16 and 10.0.1.0/24 all matching, /24 wins.
  • 0.0.0.0/0 is the default route — the catch-all.

AWS VPC

  • IGW attaches the VPC to the Internet. A public subnet simply means its route table has 0.0.0.0/0 → igw-…; instances can be reached both ways.
  • NAT Gateway lives in a public subnet. A private subnet routes 0.0.0.0/0 → nat-…: updates flow out, attackers cannot flow in.
  • Both tables keep 10.0.0.0/16 → local. One word in one row (igw vs nat) changes who reaches the Internet — and who reaches you.
Exam trap: the NAT Gateway sits in the public subnet, but the route that points to it is in the private subnet’s table.

IP’s control & error channel

  • Carries control and error messages for IP, never user data.
  • Lives inside IP datagrams (type · code · reason) yet is a network-layer helper: travels in IP, reports on IP.
  • A router that drops a packet can say why — root of diagnostics.

Messages

  • Echo Request / Reply — “are you there?” → “yes”; powers ping.
  • Time Exceeded — TTL hit 0, router drops and reports; powers traceroute.
  • Destination Unreachable — no route, host down or port closed.
  • Redirect — “there is a better next hop”, sent by a router to a host.

ping

  • Echo request out → echo reply back: confirms reachability and measures round-trip time (RTT).
  • Fast reply = reachable (farther host, bigger RTT). Total silence (“Request timed out”, 100% loss) = nothing answered — your first question in any outage.

traceroute — the TTL trick

  • Send packets with TTL = 1, 2, 3 …; each router that hits 0 returns Time Exceeded, revealing itself one hop per round.
  • Rising RTTs = each hop farther. * * * = no reply (filtered or dropped, not always broken).
  • The last responding hop is your prime suspect: replies through hop 6 then * * * → investigate at or just after hop 6.

Why

IPv4 is 32 bits: 2³² ≈ 4.3 billion addresses — fewer than people on Earth. NAT was a patch that bought time, not more addresses.

IPv6 basics

  • 128 bits = 8 groups × 4 hex digits (16 bits each) ≈ 3.4 × 10³⁸ addresses.
  • :: shortcut: drop leading zeros, then collapse one run of zero groups. 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001 → 2001:db8::1. :: may appear only once (otherwise the zero count is ambiguous).

What changes

  • No NAT needed — end-to-end addressing returns.
  • Simplified header — fixed format, faster for routers.
  • Autoconfiguration — hosts self-assign; no DHCP server required.
  • No broadcast — uses multicast (talk to a group).

Living in a mixed world

Dual stack runs v4 and v6 together (prefers v6, falls back to v4); tunneling/translation bridge gaps. Adoption is slow because NAT “worked well enough”. AWS VPCs take an IPv6 CIDR alongside IPv4.

Routing = shortest path

  • Route tables decide the next hop — algorithms fill them, continuously, on every router.
  • Routers = nodes, links = edges, cost = weight (delay, hops or inverse bandwidth). Output is destination → next hop, not the full path.
  • Two approaches: global (link-state) vs decentralised (distance-vector). Analogy: Google Maps.

Link-State — everyone gets the whole map

  • Flood link-state information to all routers, then each runs Dijkstra locally on identical data.
  • Dijkstra: visited set + tentative distances (source = 0, others ∞); repeatedly pick the unvisited node with minimum distance and relax its neighbours.
  • Lecture example from A: C=1, B=3, D=4, E=7, path A–C–B–D–E.
  • Fast convergence, no loops, but more messages and CPU/memory per router. Real protocol: OSPF.

Trust only your neighbours

  • No map — each router shares its distance vector with direct neighbours periodically.
  • Bellman-Ford update: D(dest) = min over neighbours n of [ cost(to n) + D_n(dest) ]. Iterate until nothing changes.
  • Example: D_X(Z)=7; Y advertises D_Y(Z)=1 and cost(X,Y)=2 → min(7, 2+1) = 3, so X routes to Z via Y. Real protocol: RIP.

Count-to-infinity

  • Line A–B–C, cost 1 each; C routes to A via B. Link A–B fails. B should announce “A unreachable”, but C’s stale vector (cost 2 to A) reaches B first; B believes it and sets cost 3, then C goes to 4 … climbing toward ∞.
  • Root cause: routers act on second-hand information without the full map. Good news travels fast, bad news crawls.

Split horizon / poisoned reverse

  • Split horizon: never advertise a route back to the neighbour you learned it from.
  • Poisoned reverse: advertise it back as cost ∞ (“don’t use me”).
  • Fixes the simple loop but not every topology — the standard first fix.

Link-State vs Distance-Vector

  • Knowledge: whole map (LS) vs neighbours only (DV).
  • Messages: flood to everyone vs exchange vectors with neighbours.
  • Convergence: fast, no loops vs slower, can loop (count-to-infinity).
  • Scale: CPU/memory heavy vs lightweight but slow to fix failures.
  • Protocol: OSPF (Dijkstra) vs RIP (Bellman-Ford).
Next: BGP is path-vector — the Internet’s inter-domain glue (Lecture 18). Analogy: link-state = company org chart everyone sees; distance-vector = office gossip.

Network devices — the lab view

Device A

NIC — Network Interface Card

Wired (Ethernet) or wireless (Wi-Fi). Converts device data into signals for the network medium. Carries a unique MAC address — a 48-bit identifier burned in at manufacture (e.g. AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF), used for communication within the same local network (Layer 2).

Device B

Hub — Layer 1

Broadcasts to ALL ports regardless of destination. Every device sees every packet (security + performance issue). Half-duplex — only one device transmits at a time. Creates a single collision domain. Largely obsolete.

Device C

Switch — Layer 2

Learns which MAC sits on which port via a MAC Address Table (CAM table). Forwards only to the intended port (unicast). Separate collision domain per port. Supports full-duplex.

Device D

Router — Layer 3

Connects different networks, including yours to the internet. Assigns IPs (often via DHCP), maintains a routing table, provides NAT (private IPs → one public IP), and acts as the default gateway.

Device E

Modem

Modulator-Demodulator. Converts digital data from your router into analog signals for telephone/cable lines, and back. Your ISP connects to you through it. Most homes have a combined modem-router.

Device F

Access Point (AP)

Extends a wired network into a wireless one. Operates at Layer 2, communicates over Wi-Fi (802.11). Enterprises manage many APs with a wireless controller.

Key point from the lab: when you "ping" another device on your local network, the switch uses MAC addresses (not IPs) to deliver the frame at the local level.
The lab analogy: the switch is a post office inside one city (local delivery); the router is the national postal service routing between cities (inter-network delivery).

IPv4 addressing

An IPv4 address is 32 bits, written as 4 octets (e.g. 192.168.1.10). It has two parts:

  • Network portion — identifies the network (controlled by the subnet mask).
  • Host portion — identifies the specific device on that network.

Ping — Packet Internet Groper

A diagnostic tool that tests reachability and measures round-trip time (RTT). It uses ICMP (Internet Control Message Protocol) — specifically ICMP Echo Request and Echo Reply.

How ping works, step by step

  1. Your machine sends an ICMP Echo Request packet to the target IP.
  2. The target receives it and sends back an ICMP Echo Reply.
  3. Your machine records the time between sending and receiving — the RTT.
  4. This repeats 4 times (Windows) or until stopped (Mac/Linux).
Reading ping output 
64 bytes from 8.8.8.8: icmp_seq=1 ttl=113 time=12.4 ms

64 bytes  → size of the ICMP reply packet
icmp_seq=1 → sequence number of the packet
ttl=113   → Time To Live: decremented at each router hop; prevents infinite loops
time=12.4 ms → Round-trip time — lower is better

Traceroute — the TTL trick

Traceroute (tracert on Windows) reveals the path packets take, listing every router (hop) along the way. It leverages the TTL field in IP packets.

  1. Traceroute sends packets with TTL = 1. The first router decrements TTL to 0 and sends back an ICMP "Time Exceeded" message — revealing the first hop's IP.
  2. It then sends packets with TTL = 2, revealing the second hop.
  3. This continues, incrementing TTL by 1 each time, until the destination is reached.
Reading traceroute output 
1      1.2 ms      192.168.1.1     # Your home router
2      8.4 ms      100.64.0.1      # ISP gateway
3     11.2 ms      72.14.204.1     # Google upstream router
4     12.9 ms      8.8.8.8         # Destination

Each line: hop number, RTT (×3), and the IP/hostname of that router.
Asterisks (***) mean the router did not respond — often ICMP filtering by a firewall.
  • Identifies exactly where packets are being dropped or delayed.
  • High latency at a specific hop suggests congestion or a slow link at that point.
  • All hops after a certain point failing = packet drop at that router.
  • Used by ISPs and network engineers to diagnose routing issues.

What is a LAN, and how do two devices talk on one?

A LAN is a network confined to a limited physical area — a classroom, office or home. Devices share the same network prefix (e.g. 192.168.1.x) and communicate directly through the switch/router without going to the internet.

When Device A (192.168.1.10) wants to talk to Device B (192.168.1.20):

  1. Device A checks if Device B is on the same network (using the subnet mask).
  2. If yes, it uses ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) to discover Device B's MAC address.
  3. The switch forwards the frame directly to Device B's port using the MAC table.
  4. No router / internet involved — pure local communication.

SDN — the big picture

Traditional networks use hardware to enforce routing and switching rules. SDN separates the control plane (decisions about where packets go) from the data plane (actual packet forwarding). AWS implements this at massive scale — a VPC is entirely defined in software, not physical cables.

What protocol is being used — HTTP/1.1

The Python HTTP server uses HTTP/1.1 by default. Key characteristics:

  • Text-based protocol — headers and status lines are human-readable ASCII.
  • Persistent connections — Keep-Alive allows multiple requests over one TCP connection.
  • Sequential request handling — requests are processed one at a time per connection (head-of-line blocking).
  • No encryption — traffic is plaintext, visible in Wireshark.
  • Status codes: 200 OK, 404 Not Found, 500 Internal Server Error, etc.

B. 443, TCP

✓ C. chmod 600 = read/write for owner only. If the private key is compromised, an attacker can SSH into your server.

9. Common Exam Mistakes Don't do these
▼

Lecture 1 & 2 — Internet and the core

✗
Confusing bandwidth with latency. A 500 Mbps satellite link has ~480 ms RTT. Fast for downloads, unusable for conversation. "Fast" is meaningless until you say which.
✗
Thinking a faster link reduces propagation delay. It reduces transmission delay (L/R) only. Propagation is d/s — distance and physics.
✗
Unit errors in delay calculations. 2 Mbps = 2,000,000 bits/s, NOT 2. 9,000 km = 9,000,000 m, NOT 9,000. Convert before dividing. Most lost marks are here.
✗
Reporting one-way delay when asked for round trip. RTT for the satellite is ~480 ms, not ~240 ms. "Round trip" = source → destination → source, not "up and down".
✗
Adding link rates for throughput. End-to-end throughput is the minimum, not the sum or the average. 10 + 2 + 5 Mbps ⇒ 2 Mbps.
✗
Saying a router needs to know the whole path. Store-and-forward means each router only needs the next hop. No device holds a map of the internet.
✗
Trying to compute queuing delay. The syllabus explicitly puts queuing-delay maths out of scope. Name it, explain why it is variable, move on.
✗
Saying satellites carry most intercontinental traffic. ~99% goes through ~600 submarine cable systems. And sharks are not the main cause of damage — anchors and fishing gear are.

Lecture 3 — Layers

✗
Mixing up Layer 2 and Layer 3. L2 = one hop, MAC addresses, error checking. L3 = the whole journey, IP addresses, routing between different networks.
✗
Getting the PDU ladder wrong. Data → Segment (L4) → Packet (L3) → Frame (L2) → Bits (L1). Do not call an L2 frame a "packet".
✗
Reversing OSI and TCP/IP facts. OSI = 7 layers, ISO, conceptual. TCP/IP = 4 layers, DARPA, actually used. Swapping ISO and DARPA is the classic slip.
✗
Putting encryption only at Layer 6. Presentation-layer encryption is the OSI answer, but in practice TLS sits between the application and TCP. Read the question: if it names OSI, answer Layer 6; if it names HTTPS, answer "HTTP wrapped in TLS above TCP".
✗
Swapping protocol and service. Protocol = horizontal (peer to peer across the network). Service = vertical (what a layer offers the layer above it locally).

Lecture 4 — Devices, topologies, cloud

✗
Forgetting to divide by 2 in n(n−1)/2. 10 devices = 45 cables, not 90. Each cable connects a pair — counting from both ends double-counts.
✗
Saying a switch can reach another network. It cannot — it has no understanding of Layer 3. That is precisely why the router exists.
✗
Claiming a packet-filtering firewall inspects the data. It reads the IP header (source/dest IP) and the TCP/UDP header (source/dest port). The payload is not examined.
✗
Ranking topologies as "best". Star = medium fault tolerance / good scalability. Mesh = high / excellent but n² cabling. Hybrid = high fault tolerance but hard to scale. It is a trade-off table, not a leaderboard.
✗
Confusing Region, AZ and Edge Location. Region = geographic area containing multiple AZs. AZ = isolated fault domain (one or more data centres). Edge Location = CDN cache close to users.
✗
Mapping a router to a VPC. Router → Route Table. VPC is the whole network. Physical server → EC2. Firewall rules → Security Groups / NACLs.

Lecture 5 — HTTP & REST

✗
403 vs 404. 403 Forbidden = the server understood and refuses. 404 Not Found = the server cannot find it. And 301 = permanent redirect, 302 = temporary.
✗
Saying HTTP/2 fixes all head-of-line blocking. It fixes application-level HoL. TCP-level HoL remains — one lost segment stalls every stream. Only HTTP/3 (QUIC over UDP) fixes that.
✗
Claiming statelessness means no shopping carts. The protocol is stateless; state is carried by cookies and session tokens in the messages.
✗
Confusing the request line with the status line. Request line = Method + URL + Version. Status line = Version + Status code + Status message.
✗
Saying POST can be pipelined. Only safe methods (GET, HEAD, OPTIONS) can. POST/PUT alter destination state — they have side effects.

Lecture 6 — HTTPS & TLS

✗
Saying the padlock means the site is safe. It means encrypted + verified identity . It does not mean the site is trustworthy — phishing sites get valid certificates too.
✗
Saying TLS uses only asymmetric encryption. It uses both: asymmetric for the handshake (identity + key exchange), then fast symmetric encryption for the actual data.
✗
Thinking encryption alone stops a MITM attack. It does not — the attacker can encrypt too. Certificate validation is the step that stops MITM.
✗
Saying the session key is "sent" to the server. It is derived by both sides during key exchange. Private keys are never transmitted at all.
✗
Calling HTTPS a different protocol from HTTP. HTTPS = the same HTTP, wrapped in TLS, on port 443 instead of 80.

Labs

✗
Saying ping uses TCP. Ping uses ICMP (protocol number 1). A "request timeout" may simply mean a firewall is blocking ICMP, not that the host is down.
✗
Saying DNS is TCP-only or UDP-only. Port 53, UDP by default, TCP when the response exceeds 512 bytes or for zone transfers.
✗
Getting the private-IP Class B range wrong. It is 172.16.0.0 – 172.31.255.255, not 172.0–172.255.
✗
Forgetting why chmod 600 is mandatory. SSH refuses key files that are world-readable. It is a security measure, not a formality.
✗
Mixing up A and AAAA records. A = IPv4. AAAA = IPv6. CNAME = alias, MX = mail, NS = name server, PTR = reverse DNS.

3 marks  S1. A 12,000-bit packet is sent over a 3 Mbps link that is 1,200 km long (signal speed 2 × 10⁸ m/s). Calculate the transmission delay and the propagation delay, and state which one a link upgrade would reduce.
Transmission delay = L / R
L = 12,000 bits, R = 3 Mbps = 3,000,000 bits/s
= 12,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = 0.004 s = 4 ms

Propagation delay = d / s
d = 1,200 km = 1,200,000 m, s = 2 × 10⁸ m/s
= 1,200,000 ÷ 200,000,000 = 0.006 s = 6 ms

Which a link upgrade reduces: only the transmission delay. Propagation delay depends on distance and signal speed, neither of which bandwidth can change — you can buy more bandwidth, you cannot buy a shorter distance or a faster speed of light.
4 marks  S2. A geostationary satellite orbits 35,786 km above the equator and the link is advertised at 500 Mbps. Explain, with calculation, why a video call over this link feels laggy, and why the advertised speed is irrelevant to that problem.
One-way path: the signal must climb to the satellite and come back down ≈ 2 × 35,786 km ≈ 71,500 km = 71,500,000 m.
In free space s ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
One-way propagation delay = 71,500,000 ÷ 3 × 10⁸ ≈ 240 ms

Round trip (source → destination → source) ≈ 143,000 km ≈ 480 ms.

Why it feels laggy: what a person experiences in conversation is the round-trip time — you speak, they hear, they react, their reply returns. ~480 ms is the "awkward half-second lag" where both people talk over each other.

Why 500 Mbps is irrelevant: bandwidth and latency are independent. Bandwidth (R) only reduces transmission delay L/R; it has no effect on d/s. The link is excellent for downloading a large file and unusable for conversation.
3 marks  S3. A path from a server to a client crosses four links of 80 Mbps, 25 Mbps, 6 Mbps and 40 Mbps. (a) What is the approximate end-to-end throughput? (b) The ISP upgrades the first link to 1 Gbps. What is the new throughput? (c) Justify your answer in one sentence.
(a) End-to-end throughput ≈ min(R₁, R₂, R₃, R₄) = min(80, 25, 6, 40) = ≈ 6 Mbps. The 6 Mbps link is the bottleneck link.

(b) Still ≈ 6 Mbps — completely unchanged.

(c) A chain is governed by its weakest link: the slowest hop sets the pace for the whole path, so upgrading any non-bottleneck link changes nothing. The only useful engineering question is which hop is the slowest.
3 marks  S4. An office has 60 devices. (a) How many cables would a full mesh require? (b) How many does a star topology require? (c) What does this comparison explain about why networking hardware exists?
(a) Full mesh = n(n − 1)/2 = 60 × 59 / 2 = 3,540 / 2 … carefully: 60 × 59 = 3,540, then ÷ 2 = 1,770 cables.

(b) Star = n = 60 cables (one per device to the central switch).

(c) Devices grow linearly while full-mesh cabling grows with the square — ten times the devices costs roughly a hundred times the cabling. The pivot "connect everyone to ONE device instead of to each other" reduces n(n−1)/2 to n, and that single move is the reason networking hardware (hubs, switches, routers) exists at all.
2 marks  S5. A 70-billion-parameter model is stored at 2 bytes per parameter and an H100 GPU has 80 GB of memory. Show why training this model necessarily creates a networking problem.
Memory required = 70 × 10⁹ × 2 bytes = 140 GB.
Available on one H100 = 80 GB. Since 140 GB > 80 GB, the model does not fit on one machine.

It must therefore be split across multiple GPUs/machines — and the moment you split it, you have a network. At every training step each GPU must combine its gradients with every other GPU's result (an all-reduce) before the next step can begin, so the interconnect becomes the limiting factor rather than the compute.

5 marks  S12. Describe the TLS handshake step by step. At which step is a man-in-the-middle attack prevented, and how?
1. ClientHello — the client offers the TLS versions it supports, its cipher suites, and a random nonce (a 32-byte number used once).
2. ServerHello + Certificate — the server replies with the chosen cipher suite and its digital certificate, which contains its public key and the CA's signature.
3. Authentication — the client validates the certificate chain back to a trusted root CA. If valid, the client can trust the server; if not, the browser shows that the website is not secure.
4. Key exchange — client and server derive a shared symmetric session key (it is never transmitted in the clear).
5. Secure channel — communication begins, with encrypted application data exchanged using that session key.

Where MITM is prevented: step 3. Encryption alone does not help — an attacker can encrypt a connection to you perfectly well, which just means you are securely talking to the wrong person. The attacker cannot obtain a trusted CA's signature on a certificate for the real domain, so certificate validation fails and the browser refuses. Identity, not encryption, is what stops MITM.
5 marks  S13. Trace the complete DNS resolution process for google.com when nothing is cached. State which transport protocol and port are used and why.
1. Browser cache — the browser checks whether it resolved this domain recently.
2. OS cache — the operating system checks its local DNS cache.
3. Recursive resolver — the query goes to your ISP's or configured DNS server (e.g. 8.8.8.8), which does the work on your behalf.
4. Root name server — the resolver asks a root server, which responds with the address of the TLD server.
5. TLD name server — the .com TLD server is queried; it returns the authoritative server for google.com.
6. Authoritative name server — the server that actually holds google.com's records responds with the IP address.
7. Caching — the resolver caches the result for the TTL duration.
8. Connection — the browser connects to the returned IP.

Transport and port: DNS uses port 53 for both UDP and TCP. UDP is the default — it is fast, connectionless and low-overhead, which suits small queries under 512 bytes. TCP is used when the response exceeds 512 bytes (DNSSEC records, zone transfers between servers), or after a UDP response arrives with the truncation (TC) flag set.

Timing: the whole process typically completes in 10–100 ms.
5 marks  S14. Explain encapsulation and decapsulation. Name the PDU produced at each layer as data travels down the sender's stack.
Encapsulation works like a set of nesting dolls: as data moves down the sender's stack, each layer wraps the data handed to it from above with its own header. Decapsulation reverses this on the receiver: each layer strips off its own header and hands the remaining payload upward. Equivalently, you wrap a gift in paper, then a box, then ribbon — and unwrap in exactly the reverse order. You cannot untie the ribbon last.

The PDU ladder going down:
· Application / Presentation / Session (L7-5) → Data
· Transport (L4) → Segment (TCP) or Datagram (UDP) — adds source/destination port numbers
· Network (L3) → Packet — adds source/destination IP addresses and TTL
· Data Link (L2) → Frame — adds source/destination MAC addresses and an error-check trailer
· Physical (L1) → Bits on the medium

The key property is symmetry: Layer 3 on the receiver reads exactly the header Layer 3 on the sender wrote, and neither touches anything belonging to another layer — each layer holds a private conversation with its counterpart at the other end. This is why a real packet capture in Wireshark shows a series of nested headers followed by your actual data.
4 marks  S15. Explain how traceroute discovers every router along a path. Why does the output sometimes show asterisks?
Traceroute exploits the TTL (Time To Live) field in the IP header, which every router decrements by one.

1. Traceroute sends a packet with TTL = 1. The first router decrements it to 0, discards the packet, and returns an ICMP "Time Exceeded" message — revealing hop 1's IP address.
2. It then sends TTL = 2, so the second router returns Time Exceeded — revealing hop 2.
3. This repeats, incrementing TTL by 1 each round, until the destination is reached.

Each output line shows the hop number, the RTT (×3), and the IP/hostname of that router.

Why asterisks (***) appear: that router did not respond — most often because a firewall is filtering ICMP. It does not necessarily mean the path is broken; packets may still be flowing through that hop.

Diagnostic value: high latency at one specific hop indicates congestion or a slow link there, and all hops failing after a certain point indicates packets are being dropped at that router.
4 marks  S16. List, in order, everything that happens between typing https://example.com and the page rendering. Name the protocol involved at each stage.
1. DNS resolution — the name example.com is translated to an IP address. Protocol: DNS, port 53, normally over UDP.
2. TCP three-way handshake — SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK establishes a reliable connection to port 443. Protocol: TCP (Layer 4).
3. TLS handshake — ClientHello → ServerHello + Certificate → certificate validation → key exchange. Protocol: TLS.
4. HTTP request — GET / HTTP/1.1 with headers, sent inside the encrypted channel. Protocol: HTTP (Layer 7).
5. HTTP response — HTTP/1.1 200 OK with headers and the HTML body, travelling back edge → access network → core → access network → edge.
6. Rendering, then repetition — the browser parses the HTML and issues many further requests for CSS, JS, images and fonts; a single real page can produce over a hundred requests across a dozen domains, each repeating steps 1–5.

Note: steps 1–3 all complete before a single byte of page content arrives — which is why latency, not bandwidth, dominates how fast a page feels.
3 marks  S17. Explain the store-and-forward rule and state one advantage and one cost of it.
Rule: a router must receive the entire packet before it sends even the first bit onward. Receive fully → check → forward fully.

Advantage: no single router needs to know the whole path — it only needs to know the next hop. This is what makes a network of the internet's size governable at all: no device anywhere holds a complete map of the internet. It also lets the router verify the packet for bit errors before propagating it.

Cost: every hop adds a full transmission delay (L/R) before forwarding can begin. This is also why very large packets are not simply better — across many hops, huge packets mean long waits at each hop, while tiny packets waste the link on headers and per-packet processing. Real networks settle this trade-off with a maximum transmission unit (MTU).

5 marks  S18. A user pays for a 300 Mbps connection but their 4K stream buffers every evening around 10 PM. Analyse where the bottleneck could be, and explain why upgrading their plan may not help.
The full path is: content server → congested inter-network (peering) link → the user's ISP → home WiFi → the TV.

The misunderstanding: the 300 Mbps figure describes only the access link between the user and their ISP — one stop on a long path. End-to-end throughput ≈ the minimum link rate anywhere on the path, so if any other hop is slower, the plan size is irrelevant.

Candidate bottlenecks, in order of likelihood:
1. Congestion at the peering point where the ISP's network meets the content provider's — most likely at 10 PM, when everybody streams at once. Note that content providers already place caching appliances inside ISP networks so the film starts a few kilometres away rather than on another continent; when it still stalls, the interconnect is the suspect.
2. The user's own home WiFi, struggling with walls and interference — very often the real culprit.
3. An overloaded server at the far end.

Why upgrading the plan may not help: it widens a link that was never the bottleneck. A chain is governed by its weakest link — the useful question is always which hop is the slowest.

Worth adding for the extra mark: who pays to upgrade a congested peering link is a commercial negotiation, not an engineering one — the Netflix–Comcast dispute of 2014 is the classic case. Networks are economics and contracts as well as wires and protocols.
5 marks  S19. A student runs python3 -m http.server 8000 on their laptop. Their classmate on the same WiFi cannot open http://<laptop-IP>:8000, though the server is clearly running. Diagnose systematically.
Step 1 — is the host's own stack alive? ping 127.0.0.1 on the laptop. Failure indicates a NIC or OS problem.
Step 2 — are they actually on the same LAN? Both machines should share the same network prefix (e.g. 192.168.1.x). Confirm the IP with ipconfig / ifconfig / ip addr. A common mistake is sharing a public IP, or being on a guest network that isolates clients.
Step 3 — is the host reachable at all? The classmate runs ping <laptop-IP>. If ping fails but they are on the same subnet, the problem is at Layer 2/3 — ARP, the switch, or client isolation on the access point.
Step 4 — the most likely answer. If ping succeeds but the browser times out, the host's firewall is blocking inbound connections on port 8000. A firewall makes its decision on source/destination IP (Layer 3) and source/destination port (Layer 4) — port 8000 simply is not permitted.

What is not the problem:
· Not DNS — an IP address was used, so no name resolution is involved.
· Not a privilege issue — only ports below 1024 are privileged; 8000 needs no sudo.

Note: once it works, the request travels through the switch to the laptop, Python's server responds with the HTML, and the classmate's browser renders it — a complete client-server exchange over a LAN, with no router or internet involved.
5 marks  S20. An EC2 instance is in the "Running" state. You can SSH into it and confirm a web server is listening on port 80, but the site does not load in a browser. Identify the cause and explain the reasoning.
Diagnosis: the Security Group has no inbound rule allowing HTTP on port 80.

Reasoning — what the working SSH already rules out:
· The instance is running and has a public IP, otherwise SSH could not reach it.
· Port 22 is open in the Security Group, so the SG exists and is attached.
· The .pem permissions are correct (chmod 600), else SSH would have refused the key.
· The route table and Internet Gateway are working, since traffic reached the instance from outside the VPC.
· The server process is listening, as confirmed on the instance.

Everything on the path therefore works except the rule permitting port 80.

Fix: edit the Security Group's inbound rules to allow HTTP (TCP port 80) from 0.0.0.0/0.

Concept to name for the mark: a Security Group is a virtual firewall attached to an instance, filtering on protocol, port and source IP — exactly the packet-filtering behaviour of a physical firewall, instantiated in software. It is stateful (an allowed inbound request's reply is automatically permitted out), unlike a NACL, which sits at the subnet boundary and is stateless.

Also worth noting: if the server were started as python3 -m http.server 80 without sudo, it would have failed to bind, because ports below 1024 are privileged.
5 marks  S21. A company's web application is slow for users in Australia but fast in India, where it is hosted. The team proposes buying more bandwidth. Evaluate this proposal and recommend a better approach.
Evaluating the proposal: buying bandwidth will not fix this.
Bandwidth (R) only affects transmission delay = L/R. The dominant delay here is propagation delay = d/s, set by the physical distance between Australia and India and the speed of light in fibre (≈ 2 × 10⁸ m/s). No commercial upgrade changes either term.

The correct approach: reduce d, not increase R.
· Deploy Edge Locations / a CDN that cache content physically closer to the user, so the content starts a few hundred kilometres away instead of on another continent.
· Deploy into an AWS Region in or near Australia — a Region being an isolated geographic area containing multiple data-centre clusters, joined to the others by an ultra-low-latency backbone.
· Use multiple Availability Zones within that Region for fault tolerance, so the deployment survives a fire, flood or power failure at one site.

The general principle: this is exactly why Google, Meta and Netflix lay their own private fibre and place caching appliances inside ISP networks. They are not buying bandwidth — they are buying distance.
4 marks  S22. A user sees the padlock icon on micrоsоft.com (with Cyrillic "о" characters) and enters their password. Explain exactly what TLS did and did not guarantee here.
What TLS DID guarantee:
· Confidentiality — the connection was genuinely encrypted; no eavesdropper could read the password in transit.
· Integrity — the data was not altered in transit.
· Authentication — the certificate correctly proved that the server really is the owner of the domain micrоsоft.com, and the chain validated back to a trusted root CA.

What TLS did NOT guarantee: that the website is safe or trustworthy.
The padlock answers three questions: Encrypted connection? · Verified identity? · Safe website?

The identity that was verified is the attacker's own domain. Because the Cyrillic characters form a genuinely different domain name, the attacker could legitimately obtain a valid certificate for it. Nothing in the protocol failed — the user's assumption failed.

The lesson: TLS proves you are securely talking to whoever owns the name in the address bar. It cannot tell you whether that name is the one you meant.
4 marks  S23. A small office of 40 machines is currently connected with hubs and users complain the network "gets slower as more people join". Explain the cause in networking terms and recommend a fix.
Cause: unnecessary traffic. A hub operates at Layer 1 and does not recognise MAC addresses at all — it simply repeats the signal arriving on one port out of every other port. Consequences:
· Every machine must receive, inspect and discard traffic never meant for it.
· While that traffic occupies the shared medium, nobody else can transmit — all ports share a single collision domain, and hubs are half-duplex.
· Therefore the problem worsens with every device added, which is exactly the symptom reported.

Fix: replace the hubs with switches, in a star topology.
· A switch operates at Layer 2 and learns which MAC address sits behind which port (its MAC/CAM table), so it forwards each frame only to the correct port (unicast).
· Each port becomes its own collision domain and supports full-duplex.
· A star topology needs only n = 40 cables (versus 40 × 39 / 2 = 780 for a full mesh), and its centralised structure simplifies diagnostics and maintenance.

Trade-off to acknowledge for the final mark: the central switch becomes a single point of failure — star has only medium fault tolerance, which is the mirror image of the property that makes it easy to manage.

2 marks  S24. Define statistical multiplexing and state the two advantages it gives.
Definition: sharing a link on demand, based on the statistics of how users actually behave, rather than reserving capacity for the worst case. Links are used only when packets actually need them.

Efficiency — real traffic is bursty, so many users can share one link because it is statistically unlikely they all burst at the same instant.
Resilience — because packets are not tied to one fixed path, traffic can be rerouted around a failed link or router; nothing was reserved there, so nothing is lost when it disappears.
2 marks  S25. Why is queuing delay described as "variable", and what visible effect does it cause?
Queuing delay is the time a packet spends waiting in a router's buffer behind other packets. It is the only delay that depends on what everyone else is doing rather than on your own packet or link, so it changes moment to moment with congestion.

Visible effect: jitter — variation in the delay between successive packets, which is what makes voice and video calls stutter even when average latency looks acceptable. (Its mathematics is outside this course's scope; it is named and reasoned about, not computed.)
3 marks  S26. What is layering, and what four benefits does it provide?
Layering divides network communication into levels where each layer solves one problem and speaks only to the layers directly above and below it, deliberately knowing nothing about the rest. The postal analogy: you write the letter, the carrier reads only the address — neither needs to understand the other's job, and the letter still arrives.

Benefits:
1. Each layer solves one problem and can be understood on its own.
2. A layer can be replaced without disturbing the others — swap WiFi for Ethernet and nothing above needs rewriting.
3. Different vendors can build different layers and still interoperate, because the boundaries are agreed in advance.
4. When something breaks, the fault can be localised to a layer instead of searching the whole system.
3 marks  S27. Distinguish a protocol from a service, with an example of each.
A protocol is horizontal. It is the set of agreed rules and message formats between peer layers on two different machines. Layer 4 on your laptop speaks to Layer 4 on the server according to TCP's rules. Examples: IP, TCP, HTTP, TLS, DNS.

A service is vertical. It is what a layer offers to the layer directly above it on the same machine. Example: the Network layer offers "get this packet to that IP address" to the Transport layer; the Transport layer offers "a reliable, ordered byte stream" to the Application layer.

Memory hook: protocols go across the network; services go up and down your own stack.
2 marks  S28. What is packet filtering, and which parts of a packet does a simple packet-filtering firewall examine?
Packet filtering is a firewall deciding whether to allow or block each packet based on header fields, without inspecting its contents.

It examines exactly four values:
· from the IP header (Layer 3) — the source and destination IP address
· from the TCP/UDP header (Layer 4) — the source and destination port number

The data/payload is not examined. In other words, a packet-filtering firewall reads two layers deep and stops there; higher-grade firewalls inspect further.
2 marks  S29. HTTP is stateless. Explain what that means and how shopping carts still work.
Stateless means each request–response exchange is independent — the server remembers nothing about the client between requests. Every request must carry everything the server needs to handle it.

Carts persist because the state is carried in the messages, not in the protocol: the server issues a cookie or session token, the browser stores it and returns it with every subsequent request, and the server uses it to look up the cart. The protocol stays stateless; the application keeps the state.
3 marks  S30. State the three core goals of cryptography and how TLS delivers each.
1. Confidentiality (privacy) — information is accessible only to authorised parties. "No eavesdropping." Delivered by symmetric encryption of the session using the shared session key.
2. Integrity — data remains accurate and unaltered in transit or storage. "No tampering." Delivered by integrity/message-authentication checks on every TLS record.
3. Authentication — the identities of sender and receiver are verified. "Verified identity." Delivered by the server's digital certificate, signed by a Certificate Authority and validated back along the chain of trust.
3 marks  S31. Explain the difference between a Region, an Availability Zone and an Edge Location in AWS, and give the reason each exists.
Region — an isolated geographic area (e.g. Mumbai) containing multiple data-centre clusters; there are 30+ globally, joined by an ultra-low-latency backbone. Exists for geographic choice, data residency and proximity to users.

Availability Zone (AZ) — one or more discrete data centres within a Region, with redundant power, networking and connectivity; an isolated fault domain. Exists for redundancy — deploying across two or more AZs means the application survives a fire, flood or power-grid failure at any one site.

Edge Location — a CDN endpoint that caches content physically closer to the user. Exists for latency: it shortens d in d/s, reducing propagation delay.

Relationship: a Region contains multiple AZs; Edge Locations are far more numerous than Regions and sit outside them, close to users.

A VPC (Virtual Private Cloud) is a logically isolated, software-defined network inside the AWS cloud, in which you control the IP address ranges (in CIDR notation), the subnets and the routing. It is conceptually identical to a physical LAN, but implemented entirely in software — an instance of Software-Defined Networking (SDN).

2 marks  S33. Why does the Data Link layer exist separately from the Network layer?
They solve different-sized problems. The Data Link layer (L2) worries only about one hop — getting a frame safely across a single direct link, using MAC addressing, media access control ("one at a time!") and error detection. The Network layer (L3) worries about the whole journey — logical addressing and routing across different networks.

The separation is what allows a network of networks to exist: Layer 3 stops caring whether the next link is Ethernet, WiFi or fibre, so any link technology can be swapped in beneath it without changing anything above.
3 marks  S34. Explain head-of-line blocking, and state how HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 each address it.
Head-of-line blocking occurs when responses on one connection must be delivered in request order (FIFO), so a slow item at the front of the queue blocks everything behind it — even resources that are already ready. In HTTP/1.1, a slow image will hold up the CSS, JS and icons queued behind it.

HTTP/2 introduces multiplexing over a single TCP connection with a binary framing layer, so requests and responses interleave freely. This fixes application-level HoL blocking — but TCP-level HoL remains, because TCP guarantees in-order delivery of the whole byte stream, so one lost segment stalls every stream on that connection.

HTTP/3 runs on QUIC over UDP, where streams are truly independent. A lost packet affects only its own stream; the rest continue. This is what finally solves head-of-line blocking.

The diagnostic shortcut: what a working SSH already proves

If you can SSH into the instance, then all of these are already correct: the instance is running · it has a public IP · the route table has an IGW route · the IGW is attached · the NACL permits traffic · the .pem permissions are right · port 22 is open in the Security Group.

So if SSH works but HTTP does not, the fault is almost certainly one of exactly two things: the Security Group has no rule for port 80, or the web server is not actually listening.

Run these on the instance 
sudo ss -tlnp                  # Is anything LISTENING on :80?

· Connection refused → the packet arrived, but nothing was listening on that port. Network path is fine; the service is the problem.

5 marks  A2. (a) 10.0.1.0/24 is a public subnet — its route table contains 0.0.0.0/0 → igw-abc. 10.0.2.0/24 is a private subnet — it has no default route to the internet. A subnet is public because of its route table, not its name.

(b) Yes. Both route tables contain 10.0.0.0/16 → local, so all traffic within the VPC's CIDR is routed internally. The web server can reach the database provided the database's Security Group allows inbound on the DB port (e.g. 3306) from the web server's security group or subnet CIDR.

(c) No. The private subnet has no route to the internet in either direction, so yum update would fail.

(d) Deploy a NAT Gateway in the public subnet (10.0.1.0/24) and add 0.0.0.0/0 → nat-xxxx to the private subnet's route table. This gives the database outbound-only internet access — it can fetch updates, but nothing on the internet can initiate a connection to it, so it stays unreachable from outside.
4 marks  A3. A teammate reports that curl http://<PUBLIC_IP> returns "Connection refused", while yesterday it returned "Connection timed out". What changed, and what should they check now?
What changed: the network path is now working.

· "Connection timed out" means the packets never got a reply — they were dropped by the Security Group, the NACL, or a missing route/IGW. That was yesterday's fault.
· "Connection refused" means the packets arrived at the instance and were actively rejected — the host is reachable, but nothing is listening on port 80.

So somebody fixed the firewall/routing, and the remaining problem is the service, not the network.

What to check now, on the instance:
1. sudo ss -tlnp — is anything actually listening on :80?
2. curl http://localhost — does it work locally?
3. Was the server started with sudo? Port 80 is privileged (below 1024); without sudo the bind fails silently and nothing listens.
4. Did the SSH session end? Killing the terminal kills a foreground python3 -m http.server process.
3 marks  A4. Your Security Group has an inbound rule allowing HTTP on port 80 but no outbound rules at all. Will a browser request succeed? Answer the same question for a NACL configured the same way.
Security Group: YES, it succeeds. Security Groups are stateful — if inbound traffic is allowed, the corresponding response is automatically permitted out, regardless of outbound rules. (In practice AWS's default outbound rule allows everything anyway.)

NACL: NO, it fails. NACLs are stateless — inbound and outbound are evaluated completely independently. An inbound allow on port 80 lets the request in, but the response leaves from port 80 to the client's ephemeral port (typically 1024–65535), and with no outbound rule permitting that range, the reply is dropped. The connection hangs and eventually times out.

The exam sentence: stateful = write one rule; stateless = write both, and remember the ephemeral port range on the return path.
3 marks  A5. Explain how this AWS round's concepts map back onto Lectures 2, 3 and 4. Name one link for each.
Lecture 2 (delay and throughput): Edge Locations cache content closer to users, shortening d in d/s and so reducing propagation delay. Choosing a Region near your users is the same move. AWS's "ultra-low-latency backbone" between Regions is the cloud version of Google and Netflix laying private fibre — buying distance, not bandwidth.

Lecture 3 (layers): a Security Group filters on IP (Layer 3) and port (Layer 4) and never inspects the payload — it is packet filtering, exactly as defined at those layers. Route tables perform Layer 3 forwarding with longest-prefix match.

Lecture 4 (devices and the cloud): the whole physical-to-cloud mapping — router → route table, firewall → security group, physical server → EC2, physical network → VPC. This is Software-Defined Networking: the control plane (where packets should go) is separated from the data plane (forwarding them), so a complete network topology is defined in software rather than cables. The concepts do not change; only how you instantiate them does.

The concept tag — 1 mark, 10 seconds, do it early

Both sample papers end with a "concept tag": a tag whose value is your answer to a networking question, entered as digits only — no spaces, no units, no punctuation.

Sample 1 — csai321:defaultrules on the instance: "The number of inbound rules a brand-new security group has before you add any."
0

A newly created security group has no inbound rules at all — the default posture is deny all inbound. (It does have one outbound rule allowing all traffic, but the question asks about inbound.) This is the "allow-list only, deny by default" behaviour that distinguishes a Security Group from a NACL.
Sample 2 — csai321:hosts on the VPC: "The number of usable host addresses in 10.20.1.0/24."
Two defensible answers — read the reasoning and decide, because I cannot see the grader's key.

251 — the AWS answer (what I would enter). A /24 has 2⁸ = 256 addresses. AWS reserves 5 in every subnet: the network address, the VPC router, DNS, one reserved for future use, and the broadcast address. 256 − 5 = 251.

254 — the generic-networking answer. Standard subnetting: 2⁸ − 2 = 254, subtracting only the network and broadcast addresses.

Why I lean 251: the tag sits on an AWS VPC, the CIDR named is an AWS subnet you just created, and the sibling concept tag in the other paper (defaultrules = 0) is an AWS-specific fact rather than a generic networking one. That pattern suggests these tags test AWS behaviour.

It is 1 mark out of 20. Enter your best answer in ten seconds and move on — do not spend three minutes on it.
